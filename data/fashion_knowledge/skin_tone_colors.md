# Skin Tone and Color Coordination

This guide explains how skin tone and undertone can inform color choices as a styling concept. It is not a measure of beauty or health. All color suggestions are starting points to experiment with, and personal preference matters more than any rule.

## What Skin Tone Means in Fashion Styling

- Skin tone is the visible depth of a person's complexion, from lighter to deeper.
- Undertone is the subtle warm, cool or neutral cast beneath the surface color.
- Stylists use both as reference points when suggesting colors, because clothing near the face can make a complexion appear brighter, softer or more contrasted.
- Skin tone and undertone are independent. Two people with a similar depth can have different undertones.
- No skin tone is better or worse. This guide describes how color relationships behave, not who should wear what.
- The app's profile values are `very_light`, `light`, `medium`, `tan`, `brown` and `deep`. `very_light` and `light` map to the Fair / Light family below, `medium` and `tan` to Medium and Tan, and `brown` and `deep` to Deep / Dark.

## Undertone as a Styling Concept

### Warm Undertone
- Often described as a golden, peachy or yellow-leaning cast.
- Colors that many people with warm undertones like to try: camel, mustard, rust, olive, coral, warm reds, gold.

### Cool Undertone
- Often described as a pink, red or blue-leaning cast.
- Colors that many people with cool undertones like to try: navy, emerald, plum, cool pinks, berry, silver.

### Neutral Undertone
- A balance of warm and cool, or a shade that is hard to categorize.
- People with neutral undertones often find a wide range of colors workable.

### Cautions
- Undertone is difficult to judge from a photo or a screen.
- Lighting, makeup, tanning and camera settings can all change what appears in an image.
- Treat any undertone judgment as tentative and offer to ask the user.

## Fair / Light Skin Tones

### General Direction
- Colors that offer moderate contrast can look defined, while very pale tones can feel soft or low-contrast against the complexion.

### Colors to Experiment With
- Neutrals: navy, charcoal, soft grey, cream, camel.
- Reds: cherry, wine, raspberry, coral.
- Blues: cobalt, royal blue, navy, teal.
- Greens: emerald, forest green, olive.
- Yellows: soft butter, mustard as an accent.
- Pinks: rose, dusty pink, fuchsia.
- Purples: lavender, plum, amethyst.
- Browns: chocolate, caramel, taupe.
- Metallics: silver, rose gold, soft gold.

## Medium Skin Tones

### General Direction
- Medium skin tones are often flexible and can work with both saturated and muted colors.

### Colors to Experiment With
- Neutrals: white, ivory, olive, beige, navy, grey.
- Reds: brick red, crimson, maroon, tomato.
- Blues: teal, peacock blue, denim, cobalt.
- Greens: bottle green, sage, emerald, moss.
- Yellows: mustard, marigold, golden yellow.
- Pinks: coral pink, magenta, watermelon.
- Purples: plum, violet, aubergine.
- Browns: camel, cinnamon, coffee.
- Metallics: gold, antique brass, silver, bronze.

## Tan Skin Tones

### General Direction
- Warm, earthy and jewel tones often show clear definition against tan skin, and light colors can look crisp.

### Colors to Experiment With
- Neutrals: white, cream, olive, khaki, charcoal.
- Reds: deep red, terracotta, brick, chili.
- Blues: cobalt, turquoise, teal, royal blue.
- Greens: emerald, forest, lime as an accent.
- Yellows: golden yellow, saffron, mustard.
- Pinks: hot pink, fuchsia, coral.
- Purples: royal purple, violet, magenta.
- Browns: rich chocolate, tan, cognac.
- Metallics: gold, copper, bronze, silver.

## Deep / Dark Skin Tones

### General Direction
- Both saturated jewel tones and bright colors can create strong contrast, while soft pastels offer a gentle, tonal effect.

### Colors to Experiment With
- Neutrals: white, ivory, camel, grey, black, navy.
- Reds: true red, crimson, wine, burgundy.
- Blues: cobalt, electric blue, royal blue, teal.
- Greens: emerald, kelly green, bottle green, olive.
- Yellows: golden yellow, sunflower, mustard.
- Pinks: fuchsia, hot pink, blush.
- Purples: royal purple, orchid, plum, violet.
- Browns: chocolate, cognac, warm camel.
- Metallics: gold, bronze, copper, silver.

## Contrast Between Clothing and Complexion

### High Contrast with Complexion
- A very light garment against deeper skin, or a very dark garment against lighter skin, creates a crisp, defined look.

### Medium Contrast with Complexion
- Mid-tone colors create a balanced and versatile look.

### Low Contrast with Complexion
- Colors close to the skin tone create a soft, tonal effect. This can look elegant but may feel understated.

### Practical Use
- Some users prefer a strong contrast; others prefer a softer effect. Ask which they like.
- Color placed near the face has the largest effect, so a scarf, dupatta or collar can be changed without changing the whole outfit.

## How Lighting Changes Perceived Color

- Daylight, warm indoor bulbs, fluorescent lights and evening lighting all shift how colors and skin appear.
- Outdoor daylight tends to show colors most accurately; warm lighting adds yellow and orange tones.
- Screens and photos change colors through brightness, filters and camera processing.
- Fabric texture matters. Silk and satin reflect light and look more saturated; matte cotton and linen look softer.
- Suggest the user try garments in the lighting where they will be worn when possible.

## Treating Recommendations as Starting Points

- No color is universally suitable or unsuitable for any skin tone.
- Style, mood, culture, comfort and occasion all matter alongside complexion.
- A user's favorite color, even if it falls outside a suggested list, is often the right choice.
- Encourage users to try colors near the face and see how they feel.
- If a user asks whether a color "suits" them, describe how it interacts with their complexion in neutral terms and defer to their preference.

## Styling Guidelines for an AI Stylist

- Use skin tone and undertone as one input alongside occasion, budget, fabric and personal preference.
- Present two or three color options rather than one.
- Describe effects in terms of contrast, warmth and softness rather than judgments.
- Do not make claims about skin health or condition.
- Do not describe any complexion as more attractive than another.
- Encourage the user to override suggestions whenever they prefer a different color.
