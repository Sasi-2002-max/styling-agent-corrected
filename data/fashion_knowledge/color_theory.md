# Color Theory for Fashion

This document explains how colors relate to each other and how those relationships can guide outfit building. Everything here is a guideline. Personal taste, culture, occasion and context can all justify choices that break these patterns.

## Basic Color Concepts

### Primary Colors
- Red, blue and yellow are the traditional primary colors.
- They are the starting point from which other hues are described.
- In clothing, pure primaries feel bold and graphic, so they are often used as a single statement piece.

### Secondary Colors
- Green, orange and purple are made by mixing two primaries.
- Green (blue + yellow) suits both casual and formal clothing in deeper shades such as bottle green.
- Orange (red + yellow) is common in festive Indian clothing, in shades from saffron to rust.
- Purple (red + blue) ranges from soft lilac to deep plum.

### Tertiary Colors
- Tertiary colors sit between a primary and a secondary color, for example teal, chartreuse, vermilion and magenta.
- They often feel more sophisticated than pure primaries and work well as accent colors.

### Warm Colors
- Reds, oranges, yellows and warm browns feel energetic and inviting.
- Warm colors tend to appear closer and more prominent in an outfit.
- Examples: mustard, terracotta, maroon, rust, camel.

### Cool Colors
- Blues, greens and purples feel calm and composed.
- Cool colors tend to recede and can feel more formal or understated.
- Examples: navy, emerald, slate, lavender, teal.

### Neutral Colors
- Black, white, grey, beige, cream, tan, brown, navy and olive are widely used as neutrals.
- Neutrals are easy to combine and are useful for balancing brighter colors.
- Some neutrals lean warm (cream, camel, chocolate) and some lean cool (charcoal, ice grey, navy).

## Color Relationships

### Complementary
- Complementary colors sit opposite each other on the color wheel, such as blue and orange, or purple and yellow.
- The pairing creates strong contrast and energy.
- It is usually easiest to wear when one color dominates and the other appears in a smaller amount.
- Example: a navy blazer with a rust or burnt-orange pocket square.

### Analogous
- Analogous colors sit next to each other on the wheel, such as blue, teal and green.
- The result looks harmonious and relaxed.
- Example: an olive shirt with a forest-green jacket and brown shoes.

### Monochromatic
- A monochromatic look uses one hue in several shades or tints.
- It creates a long, cohesive visual line and feels polished.
- Texture and fabric variation keep it from looking flat.
- Example: a light-blue shirt, mid-blue trousers and a navy jacket.

### Triadic
- Triadic schemes use three colors evenly spaced on the wheel, such as red, yellow and blue.
- They can look vibrant and playful, and work best when one color leads and the other two are toned down or used sparingly.
- Example: a mustard kurta, a teal Nehru jacket and a small touch of maroon in the stole.

### Neutral Combinations
- Combining several neutrals is a low-risk approach.
- Examples: cream and camel, grey and navy, black and white, olive and beige.
- Adding one saturated accent keeps a neutral outfit from feeling dull.

## Outfit Color Coordination

### Main Color
- The main color covers the largest area of the outfit, usually the trousers, a dress, a saree or a long coat.
- Choose it first, because the other colors are built around it.

### Secondary Color
- The secondary color appears in a second major garment, such as a shirt under a jacket.
- It should support the main color through harmony or contrast.

### Accent Color
- The accent color appears in small amounts: a scarf, dupatta border, tie, bag, shoes or jewelry.
- A small accent can lift an otherwise quiet outfit.

### Neutral Balancing
- When two or three strong colors compete, adding a neutral piece calms the look.
- A white, cream, grey or black layer often creates visual rest between bold colors.
- Neutral shoes and belts help avoid adding another competing color.

## Contrast

### High Contrast
- High contrast pairs very light with very dark tones, such as white with black or cream with navy.
- It looks sharp and graphic and suits formal or statement dressing.

### Medium Contrast
- Medium contrast pairs tones a few steps apart, such as light grey with navy or beige with olive.
- It looks balanced and suits most everyday and office settings.

### Low Contrast
- Low contrast uses tones close in depth, such as beige with cream or dusty pink with mauve.
- It looks soft and understated, and works well for relaxed, elegant or tonal outfits.

### Choosing a Contrast Level
- Higher contrast draws attention to the outfit's structure; lower contrast draws attention to texture and fit.
- The contrast between garments and between the outfit and the wearer's complexion are separate considerations. See skin_tone_colors.md for the second.

## Practical Outfit Examples

These are starting points, not fixed formulas.

### Shirts and Trousers
- White shirt with navy trousers and brown shoes: medium-to-high contrast, widely adaptable.
- Light-blue shirt with grey trousers: low-to-medium contrast, suits office settings.
- Olive shirt with beige chinos: analogous-leaning, relaxed and earthy.
- Black shirt with cream trousers: high contrast, works for evening events.

### Dresses
- A solid emerald dress with gold-toned accessories and neutral shoes.
- A floral dress: pick one color from the print for shoes or a bag.
- A black dress with a single bright accent, such as a coral bag or scarf.

### Jackets and Layers
- A navy blazer can sit over white, light blue, pink or grey.
- A camel jacket pairs with cream, olive or dark denim.
- A denim jacket over a white or striped T-shirt is a low-effort neutral-plus-blue combination.

### Shoes and Accessories
- Shoes usually work best when they relate to the belt and bag rather than to the shirt.
- Metallics (gold, silver, rose gold) act as accents; pick one metal tone per outfit as a simple default.
- A watch strap, scarf or tie is a convenient place to introduce the accent color.

## Indian Fashion Context

Indian clothing often uses richer saturation, layered colors and contrast borders. Color meaning varies by region and community, so treat any cultural association as context, not a rule.

### Sarees
- A plain silk saree with a contrast border is a natural application of a main plus accent color.
- Example: a deep green saree with a gold zari border and a maroon blouse.
- Pastel sarees pair well with a tonal or slightly deeper blouse for a low-contrast look.

### Kurtas
- Solid kurtas in cream, pastel or earthy tones pair easily with white or contrast churidars and pyjamas.
- Example: a mustard kurta with an off-white pyjama and a teal Nehru jacket.
- Printed kurtas usually work best with a solid bottom drawn from one print color.

### Sherwanis
- Ivory, cream, gold, maroon, navy and pastel shades are common choices.
- A tonal sherwani with a contrast stole or safa (turban) adds an accent.
- Embroidery color matters as much as base color, since it changes the overall impression.

### Lehengas
- Lehengas are often built on a main color with a contrast or tonal blouse and dupatta.
- Example: a deep-pink lehenga with a gold blouse and a light-pink dupatta.
- Heavily embroidered pieces usually pair best with simpler jewelry colors and quieter accessories.

### Indo-Western Outfits
- A kurta with tailored trousers, a dhoti-style skirt with a structured top, or a saree with a belt and jacket all mix silhouettes.
- Keep the palette restrained when silhouettes are mixed: two main colors plus one accent is a manageable limit.

### Western Formalwear
- Navy, charcoal, grey and black suits are conventional choices.
- White or light-blue shirts create a clean base; ties and pocket squares carry the accent color.
- For Indian weather and daytime events, lighter tones such as light grey or beige are practical alternatives.

## Styling Guidelines for an AI Stylist

- Start from the occasion and the user's stated color preferences, then use color relationships to fill in the rest.
- Build outfits with one main color, one supporting color and at most one or two accents.
- Use neutrals to stabilize an outfit when the user wants a bold color.
- Use monochromatic or analogous schemes for a calm, low-risk result.
- Use complementary or triadic schemes when the user wants a distinctive look, and keep one color dominant.
- Match contrast to formality: higher contrast tends to read as more formal, lower contrast as more relaxed.
- Offer alternatives instead of a single answer, and explain the color logic in one short sentence.
- Never present a combination as universally correct. Users' tastes, culture and setting always take priority.
